01 · The field

A parade on Station Road, and a frame for it

In brief

This treatise reads one fixed charge receivership through Tom Graves’s SCAN framework. The appointment is a small mixed-use parade: six shops, eight flats, a vacant unit, a café owner, a director who was not told, an agent, a lender and, in month five, a fire. SCAN gives a quick way to sort what is certain from what is not, and what must be done now from what can wait.

The deed is signed on a Friday. By Monday the receiver owns, in the legal sense, a responsibility for a row of shops on Station Road that he has seen once. Unit 1 is a café whose owner opens at six and knows every tenant by name. Unit 2 is a nail bar whose rent arrives in cash. Unit 3 has been empty for eleven months. Above the shops are eight flats, reached by a common stair whose light has been out since the summer. The director of the borrowing company has not been told about the appointment and will find out from a tenant. The managing agent has run the building for six years. The relationship manager at the lender wants to know what it is worth and when it will sell. The credit officer behind her wants to know what could go wrong.

Most receivership manuals treat that list as a set of tasks to be scheduled. Graves’s framework treats it as a set of things to be placed, before anything is scheduled, by asking of each one two questions: is it simple, or not; and how much time is there before it has to work? The answers sort the morning into four kinds of work that need four different kinds of tactic. Graves’s shorthand for the habit is the phrase that gave the framework its name in November 2011: let’s do a quick SCAN on this.

Why a frame at all

An experienced receiver already does something like this without naming it. They know that changing the locks on unit 3 is not like deciding whether unit 3 will let, and that neither is like the phone call at midnight when the stair is full of smoke. The trouble is that the difference is rarely written down, so it is rarely taught, and the people who inherit the file on Monday morning cannot see it. SCAN is a way of writing it down. Graves calls it a visual checklist rather than a model: it does not describe the parade, it helps sort what you know about it.

SCAN is practitioner knowledge. It was developed by Graves, an enterprise architect writing on his Tetradian weblog, over roughly a decade, and refined in public in response to readers. No peer-reviewed study of it was found in preparing this treatise. It is offered here as a working tool to be tested against cases, in the same spirit as the earlier treatises in this series: the Cynefin field kit (VI), and the inside-out reading of complex responsive processes (VII). The receivership readings, the parade and its people are this treatise’s own and should not be attributed to Graves.

Why this matters for a receiver

The first week of an appointment decides how the rest is handled. If everything is treated as a task on a timetable, the uncertain parts are either forced into rules or left until they become emergencies. A quick SCAN on the first morning gives each issue the kind of attention it needs.

03 · The quick SCAN

The quick SCAN: two questions on the first morning

In brief

Graves’s two questions give a two-by-two frame. Left to right runs certainty to uncertainty; top to bottom runs time before action, ending at NOW. The four corners are Simple, Complicated, Ambiguous and Not-known. This section places twelve issues from the parade’s first morning on that frame.

The first question, is it simple or not, is not about how important an issue is or how hard it is to do. It is about whether doing the same thing will reliably give the same result. Serving a notice on a tenant is simple in this sense: the form is fixed, the method of service is fixed, and if it is done correctly it works. Persuading the café owner to sign a new lease is not: the same letter might bring a signature or a solicitor, depending on her trade, her mood and what she has heard about receivers.

The second question, how much time do we have, measures distance from the moment of action. Insurance must be confirmed today. The sale strategy can wait for the valuation. The renewal of the café’s lease has a statutory timetable. Graves draws time as the vertical axis with NOW at the bottom, so that everything is placed relative to the point at which it has to work, not the point at which someone raised it.

The four corners

Upper left, certain and with time to think, is Graves’s Complicated domain: analysis, calculation, the valuation on two bases. Lower left, certain and close to action, is the Simple domain: rules and work-instructions, the day-one checklist. Upper right, uncertain but with time, is the Ambiguous domain: patterns, guidelines and experiments, such as a meanwhile licence to see whether anyone will trade from unit 3. Lower right, uncertain and with no time, is the Not-known: principles, skill and judgement, the night of the fire.

Press the button below to place the twelve issues one at a time. The placements are this treatise’s judgement and are there to be argued with; drag your own issues onto the frame and compare. On most parades about half of the first morning lands to the right of the red line, which is more than most receivers expect, and more than most appointment checklists allow for.

Why this matters for a receiver

A file that looks complete can still be mostly uncertain. Knowing on day one which issues are uncertain tells you where to put your most experienced people, where to run small tests, and which issues need principles agreed before anything goes wrong.

04 · The axes

The two axes, and why the lines move

In brief

The two dashed lines in SCAN are not fixed. Where certainty ends depends on who is doing the work, and where planning ends depends on how much time pressure there is. The same task can sit in a different domain for a different person on a different day.

Graves is emphatic that the boundary of effective certainty is itself uncertain. It is not a property of the task but of the task in the hands of a particular person, in a particular place, at a particular time. A rent demand to a tenant who always pays is Simple for anyone. A rent demand to the café in a slow month, which might be read as a threat, is Simple for a receiver who knows her and uncertain for a junior who does not. The task has not changed; the red line has moved.

The green line moves too. Under time pressure planning gives way to action sooner, and much of what could have been analysed has to be done. A Friday afternoon call about an escape of water in flat 4 moves the boundary up the frame: there is no time to commission a survey, only to isolate the supply and call the insurer.

Sameness and uniqueness

Graves also reads the horizontal axis as running from sameness to uniqueness. On the left, situations repeat, so rules and machines work well. On the right, each situation is in some way new, so only people with skill and judgement can handle it. A receivership is full of repeatable work (rent, notices, insurance) and full of unique moments (this director, this fire). The mistake is to manage the unique as if it were repeatable because most of the file is.

The instrument below has two sliders, for skill and time pressure, and four presets. Watch the eight tasks: they never move, but the lines do, and with them the domain each task falls in.

Why this matters for a receiver

Who is on call tonight changes what is certain tonight. A rota that puts juniors on out-of-hours cover moves much of the parade into the uncertain half of the frame, whatever the file says.

05 · The domains

Four domains, four kinds of tactic

In brief

Each SCAN domain has its own kind of tactic. Graves pairs Simple with rules, Complicated with algorithms, Ambiguous with guidelines and patterns, and Not-known with principles. His rule is short: don’t mix them up.

Graves’s names for the domains carry their meaning in their second words. The Simple domain is simple and straightforward: work that makes sense, done step by step. The Complicated domain is complicated but controllable: work that needs analysis but whose answer, once found, can be packaged and reused. The Ambiguous domain is ambiguous but actionable: work whose answer can only be found by trying things, and which may have to be re-solved each time. (Graves first called it actionable but awkward, and changed the name after a comment from Cynthia Kurtz.) The Not-known domain is not-known, none-of-the-above: everything that fits none of the others, where most human work actually happens.

In 2013 he added keywords for each domain, two beginning with R: regulation and rotation for the Simple; reciprocation and resonance for the Complicated; recursion and reflexion for the Ambiguous; reframe and rich randomness for the Not-known. Later slide decks add a verb for each: enact, evaluate, experiment, explore.

Why mixing them up fails

Each mismatch has its own failure. Rules applied in the Not-known freeze: the template letter is read to the director on the forecourt at midnight. Analysis applied in the Simple delays what should simply be done: the locks are not changed while options are weighed. Experiment applied to a statutory step makes it invalid: a notice cannot be trialled. Principles applied where a rule would do produce inconsistency: two tenants are treated differently for no reason. Graves allows one deliberate exception, a mismatch used in the Not-known as a reframe, to shake loose a new way of seeing.

Why this matters for a receiver

Most complaints about receivers, from lenders and borrowers alike, are complaints about mismatched tactics: rigidity where judgement was needed, or improvisation where a rule existed.

06 · NOW

NOW, and the edge of commitment

In brief

In 2019 Graves reframed SCAN’s time axis as before, during and after, and renamed one edge: the edge of action became the edge of commitment. Across it the rules change, there is no going back, and the work becomes personal. As the moment of action approaches, analysis and experiment become impossible and only rules or principles remain.

The 2019 post making sense of complexity set out four small changes. The top of the time axis, previously the indefinite future, became the indefinite before. A label for the indefinite after was added at the bottom. The regions were named before, during and after. And the edge between the Complicated and the Simple, previously the edge of action, became the edge of commitment.

The renaming matters because of what happens there. Before the edge, the rules are planning rules: options can be compared and reversed. After it, the rules are work-instructions. Going back means abandoning the task. The task becomes personal, something this person is doing now. And coordination with other tasks running in parallel becomes a separate job of its own. Beyond NOW is the after, which cannot be changed, only learned from.

The café’s renewal as a countdown

Take the café’s lease renewal ninety days out. There is time to analyse the lease, take advice, and test two sets of terms with the owner. At thirty days there is time for advice but not much testing. At three days there is time only to follow the procedure or, if something unexpected happens, to act on principle. The instrument below runs that countdown: watch the Complicated and Ambiguous options close one by one.

Why this matters for a receiver

Many appointment problems come from treating a decision as still open after it has crossed the edge of commitment, or from leaving it open until there is no time for anything but rules. Diarying when each open question must be answered is the simplest use of SCAN there is.

07 · The edges

The four edges and their feedback loops

In brief

In 2014 Graves concluded that the edges between the domains are almost the key to the whole frame. Each edge carries a feedback loop: something goes across, and something comes back. All four loops run at once.

Graves’s first of two SCAN notes in 2014 names four edges. The edge of action (since 2019, the edge of commitment) lies between Complicated and Simple: plan against action. Rules packaged from analysis go down into action; reports of what actually happened come back to revise the plan. The edge of uncertainty lies between Complicated and Ambiguous: analysis against experiment. Questions that analysis cannot answer go across to be tested; answers certain enough to use come back.

The edge of innovation lies between Ambiguous and Not-known: experiment against free exploration. Principles and seeds go across; ideas, news and surprises come back. Graves’s point is that anything genuinely new comes from the Not-known. The edge of panic lies between Simple and Not-known: structured process against improvisation, at the moment of action. Fears go across when the rules stop fitting; options simple enough to act on come back.

No diagonals

Graves notes that if the concern in hand is put at the centre of the frame, it blocks the diagonals: there is no direct route from Complicated to Not-known, or from Simple to Ambiguous. Every move goes through an edge. He reads incompetence as an attempted diagonal: belief imposed directly on the Not-known, or ambiguity resolved by arbitrary assertion. On the parade, the diagonal mistake is to take a valuation (Complicated) straight to the director on the forecourt at midnight (Not-known), without either a rule or a principle in between.

Why this matters for a receiver

An appointment learns only through its edges. If the agent’s reports never revise the plan, or the site never sends back surprises, the plan runs blind.

09 · The checklists

Four checklists, written before the work starts

In brief

In 2021 Graves described four checklists, one for each kind of known and unknown: action, preventive, emergency and chaos. All four have to be in place before work starts, because there is no time to write them once it is under way. The chaos checklist, a short set of principles, is the one most often missing.

The action checklist is the work-instruction: an ordered list of steps for what is fully known, each ticked before the next. On the parade it is the day-one list: notices, insurance, locks, bank account, agent’s instructions, diary dates. The preventive checklist is what most people mean by a checklist: a set of checks, not strictly in order, that stop possible problems from becoming actual ones. Is the insurance adequate? Are the fire doors closing? Are the stair lights working? Are the certificates in date?

The emergency checklist is for when a known possibility has actually happened, across the edge of panic. Its job is to remind people of the steps they forget under stress: call the fire service; get everyone out and count them; call the insurer’s emergency line; tell the lender in one line; record every time and decision. The chaos checklist is for what nobody foresaw. It holds no steps at all, only a handful of principles. Graves acknowledges that such lines can look like platitudes, and argues that they work as anchors around which meaning can form when nothing else gives guidance.

The night at the parade

The instrument below plays six moments from the appointment. On day one the action checklist runs. On the weekly visit the preventive checklist finds the stair lights out and a fire door wedged open, and the known risk is caught before it becomes actual. In month five, at ten past eleven at night, there is smoke in the stair: the emergency checklist. At twenty to midnight the director arrives with a locksmith and a journalist while the fire crew are still on site. No list has that. Only the principles help: people first, tell the truth early, act in good faith, keep a record. The next morning the lessons become new lines on the first two lists.

Why this matters for a receiver

Most firms have an action checklist for appointments, many have a preventive one, some have an emergency card. Very few have written their principles down in a form that the out-of-hours team could use at midnight.

10 · Skills

Trainee, apprentice, journeyman, master

In brief

Graves maps four levels of skill onto the four domains: the trainee to the Simple, the apprentice to the Complicated, the journeyman to the Ambiguous and the master to the Not-known. Skill grows over thousands of hours, not in a straight line: there is a long dip in the middle where performance seems to get worse.

In the 2016 skills frame the axes change. Theory runs to the top and practice to the bottom; repeatable work is to the left and non-repeatable work to the right. An arc rises from low variation, which is predictable, to high variation, which is unique. The trainee, with tens to a hundred hours, works to the rules. The apprentice, with hundreds to a thousand, learns why the rules work and can analyse. The journeyman, with thousands, judges what the situation depends on and knows how to find out. The master, with ten thousand or more, knows how much detail is enough and when to bend or break a rule.

Graves calls the apprentice level the most dangerous, because analysis brings a feeling of control without showing where it stops working. He describes the master with the image of a ship’s chief engineer who appears to do nothing until something goes wrong. Masters are rare, and only people can be masters: machines suit the trainee level well.

Round the bend

A 2015 post argues that the usual ladder of skill misses the experience of learning, which feels like one step forward and two back. Graves draws it as a labyrinth with a single path and no branches. After the basics comes beginner’s luck, then a sense of control from rules and analysis, then a stage in which performance seems to fall, then the long middle of conscious incompetence, and then what he calls the dark night of the soul, a despair that is normal and necessary, where people who quit lose everything they have put in. After it comes caring about the work for its own sake, absorption, embodied judgement, reflection with peers, and finally mastery. The instrument plays this path with a gauge of proficiency as it feels to the learner.

Why this matters for a receiver

Appointment teams are usually staffed by seniority, not by the domain of the work. The night of the fire needs a master; the weekly rent needs a trainee. A junior moving from rent collection to their first sale strategy should be told about the dip before it happens.

Key terms
Apprentice
The analytic level, confident and most at risk of overreach.
Journeyman
The core operating level of most organisations.
Master
The level that can work where nothing is certain and there is no time.
11 · Mission command

Intent down, feel up: the loop across the edge

In brief

Graves borrows two German military terms: auftragstaktik, mission command, in which intent rather than detailed orders is passed down; and fingerspitzengefühl, fingertip feel, the sense of what is actually happening on the ground. Together they make the feedback loop between plan and action.

In the 2015 frame the plan is at the top and the action at the bottom. On the certain side, guidance flows down as intent; on the uncertain side, realities flow back up as feel. Each side has a human condition. Mission command works only where people trust each other and know each other: otherwise intent is followed to the letter and no further, or people ring for permission. Fingertip feel works only where someone is actually in touch with the site: otherwise the plan runs without information. Graves adds a third concern: information filtered on its way up loses the awkward details that mattered.

His examples include the Dowding system of the Battle of Britain, in which information from the coast reached the decision-makers and decisions went back out quickly, and companies in which a simple instruction to do the right thing let local staff act in a disaster without waiting for head office.

The night of the fire

At 23:10 the out-of-hours line rings the receiver. The intent goes down in one sentence: everyone safe tonight, everyone housed by Friday. At 23:25 the agent’s negotiator is on site and the café owner has opened the café as a waiting room; the realities come back up: who is out, who is missing, who is frightened. At 23:40 the director arrives with a locksmith. The negotiator acts on the intent without ringing for permission: safety first, record everything, no confrontation. The next day the lender wants a report, and what reaches the credit committee depends on how much of the night survives the filters. Switch off the four conditions in the instrument one by one to see how each breaks the loop.

Why this matters for a receiver

A receiver cannot be on every site at midnight. What can be done in advance is to write the intent in a sentence the agent could act on, and to make sure someone is in touch with the building.

Key terms
Auftragstaktik
Mission command: passing intent, not detailed orders.
Fingerspitzengefühl
Fingertip feel: a direct sense of what is happening.
12 · Belief and faith

Belief, faith and the open loop

In brief

On SCAN’s decision-making side, belief sits in the Simple and faith in the Not-known, with assertion and use between them. Belief is fast and brittle; faith adapts but depends on skill. The danger Graves warns of is belief used as a substitute for sensing.

In Graves’s usage, belief means acting from a predefined answer: a rule, a plan, an assumption. It is quick and consistent, and it cannot check itself. Faith means acting from principles and from what is sensed now: it copes with variation but depends on the skill of whoever is exercising it, and can become blind faith if it stops sensing too. Assertion is the decision-making of the Complicated, based on analysis; use is the decision-making of the Ambiguous, based on what has been tried. Neither belief nor faith is enough alone; the skill is in knowing when to switch.

His 2018 post on the dangers of belief identifies the main trap: the belief stands in for sensing, so the loop between action and result runs open. The action repeats whether or not it still fits. He lists five warning signs: pressure to run the loop so fast that it becomes open; a self-confirming belief that shuts out contrary evidence; no means of collecting contradictory evidence; too little support for the faith side; and no structural way to switch between the two.

The café’s takings

In the instrument below the café’s takings fall steadily after the bus route moves in week 3. Under belief only, the plan assumes week 1 continues, and the gap grows unseen. With a weekly sensing check, the plan switches to faith when the gap passes a threshold, and resets. Under faith alone, the plan follows each week’s reading closely but costs judgement every week. Run all three and compare the gaps in the history.

Why this matters for a receiver

Rent plans, sale timetables and valuations are beliefs. Each needs a sensing check that could show it is wrong, and someone with the authority to switch.

Key terms
Belief
Acting from a predefined answer.
Faith
Acting from principles and present sensing.
Open loop
Action that no longer checks its own results.
13 · SMDA

Sense, make-sense, decide, act

In brief

Graves rewrites Boyd’s OODA loop as sense, make-sense, decide, act. Observing becomes sensing, inner as well as outer; orienting becomes making sense, which includes creating meaning where there is none. The loop is fractal: smaller loops run inside larger ones.

Graves’s point about the four phases is that only sensing is grounded in what has already happened. Making sense, deciding and acting all concern an imagined future, and are open to bias and wishful thinking. That is why he places the sensing of each cycle at the end of the previous one, in the after region of the time axis, where it can feed review. Closer to action, sensing, sense-making and deciding blur into the action itself.

A receivership runs at least three such loops: the appointment over a hundred days, the week, and the minute. The appointment loop senses the file, the site, the tenants and the lender’s fears, and decides a strategy. The weekly loop senses the agent’s report and the bank feed, and holds or adjusts the plan. The minute’s loop, at 23:10, senses smoke and a resident’s voice, and acts at once on a rule if one fits or a principle if not.

Why this matters for a receiver

Weekly calls often go straight to decisions. Starting with what was actually seen keeps the loop closed.

Key terms
SMDA
Sense, make-sense, decide, act.
Fractal
The same pattern at several scales.
14 · The bubble

The task bubble and the nozzle

In brief

In a 2018 post drawing on an image by Joseph Chittenden, Graves treats each task as a bubble whose width is its uncertainty and whose height is its elapsed time. Run-time capacity is a nozzle. Uncertain tasks jam there however much routine capacity there is.

Above the plan-to-action line is planning; below it is run-time. Every task has to pass through run-time capacity, the people available to resolve uncertainty while the work is being done. A narrow, certain task passes easily. A wide, uncertain one jams, and a jam in one task holds up the tasks behind it. Adding routine capacity does not help, because the nozzle is not routine capacity.

Graves suggests two remedies. One is to do just enough exploration before run-time, through virtual walkthroughs or rehearsals, to narrow the bubble; this costs planning time, making the bubble taller. The other is to widen the nozzle by keeping skilled people available at run-time, as call centres do when they route difficult calls to their most experienced staff.

Why this matters for a receiver

The planning pre-application for the upper floors and the soft-market test are the wide bubbles on the parade. If they are not explored early, they jam exchange of contracts. A receiver on call for the agent is a wider nozzle.

Key terms
Run-time
The period in which the work is actually being done.
Nozzle
Run-time capacity to resolve uncertainty.
15 · The dartboard

The decision-dartboard, and a budget in four lines

In brief

Kai Schlüter at Danfoss used SCAN as a dartboard: each project or task was thrown at the board and placed where the team agreed it stuck. Graves’s roadmapping slides extend the idea to budgets, with a different kind of budget line for each domain.

Graves reported Schlüter’s method in 2013. A team throws each item at the board and argues only until it agrees a domain, then moves on. Simple items are simply done; Complicated ones are analysed and estimated; Ambiguous ones are run as experiments in time-boxes; Not-known ones are recognised as novel and given a time-frame. Schlüter reported sorting many projects in a short session this way.

In the roadmapping slides each domain gets its own kind of budget. A Simple item, where we know how to do it, is a predefined line at a fixed cost. A Complicated item, which will require calculation, is a pre-assessed estimate with a stated variance. An Ambiguous item, where the best option has to be found, is a series of experiments. A Not-known item, where we do not know whether it is possible, is budgeted as risk, with an exit condition decided in advance.

The parade’s fee and cost budget

Throw twelve lines of the parade’s budget at the board. Notices, insurance and rent collection are fixed. The valuation, the lease report and the VAT advice are estimates. The meanwhile licence, the soft-market test, tenant engagement and the planning pre-application are experiments. The director’s threatened injunction, the stair survey after the fire and the council’s bus-route change are risk money. A paper presented to the credit committee this way tells it which costs are fixed, which are estimates, which buy information and which are risk.

Why this matters for a receiver

A single total hides the parts of the budget nobody can predict. Four lines are more honest, and let the lender set exit conditions rather than demand certainty.

16 · Story paths

Quick SCAN examples: stories as paths

In brief

Graves’s 2014 quick examples show that stories move through the domains, often in a cycle that begins and ends in the Not-known. This section draws his four examples and two from receivership as paths across the frame.

A toddler hears about a new baby brother and feels threatened without knowing why: the Not-known. He notices that toys which are banged get taken away: a pattern, the Ambiguous. He forms a plan to hit the baby so that it is removed: the Complicated. He carries it out simply: the Simple. And he is scolded, which nobody expected: back to the Not-known. A scientist’s story moves the same way, from an idea to a hypothesis to a theory to a law and then, sometimes, to a revolution. A trainee’s tale runs the other way, from step-by-step rules to theory to special cases, until the trainee sees that almost everything is a special case. The quality manager’s quest climbs from work-instruction to procedure to policy to vision and principles.

The receiver’s appointment follows the toddler’s order and then turns back: a Not-known first morning, weeks of patterns, a plan, a routine, a renewal that will not fit the template, and a fire. The lender’s quest follows the quality manager’s, from the credit manual to the workout policy to judgement on this borrower to recovery as a purpose.

Why this matters for a receiver

The plan is not the last stage of an appointment. Expect the path to return to the Not-known, and mark where it will cross the edge of panic.

17 · Variety weather

Variety weather and the imagined boundary

In brief

Graves reads Ashby’s law of requisite variety as weather. In calm conditions control-based methods work; in storms they fail and the boundary of effective certainty moves left. Managers often imagine the boundary further right than it is, and remove the slack that uncertain work needs.

In a 2012 post Graves argues that the same context can be controllable at one time and not at another, because the variety it has to absorb changes, as weather does. In calm weather, conventional methods work reasonably well, and the illusion of control holds until conditions change. In stormy weather they fail, and principles, skill and design thinking have to take over. He recommends monitoring the variety-weather as a matter of routine.

His 2014 post on efficient versus effective adds the imagined boundary. Managers who believe more is certain than really is will try to run uncertain work at full efficiency, removing slack. His example is a health trust that closed beds to save money and then spent more on placing patients elsewhere. He calls the slack that uncertain work needs requisite inefficiency, and argues for effectiveness first, with efficiency pursued within it.

Why this matters for a receiver

A falling market moves the sale strategy from the Complicated into the Ambiguous. A credit committee still working to a calm-weather timetable is managing on the imagined boundary. Cutting the agent’s site visits to save fees removes the slack that would have found the wedged fire door.

Key terms
Requisite variety
Ashby’s law: only variety can absorb variety.
Requisite inefficiency
Slack kept where the work is uncertain.
18 · Rules and principles

Rules, principles and the Inverse-Einstein test

In brief

Graves’s Inverse-Einstein test asks whether doing the same thing gives the same result. If it does, the situation belongs to order and can be governed by rules. If it does not, it needs principles, which are carried in stories rather than procedures.

The test inverts the line often attributed to Einstein about insanity and repetition: doing the same thing and expecting different results. In the ordered half of SCAN the same action does give the same result, so rules work. In the unordered half it may not, and expecting it to is the error. Graves’s 2012 post puts the distinction memorably: order is expressed in rules, and principles in story. Rules have a closed scope and say what to do; principles have an open scope and say what matters.

He uses the layering of quality systems to show how the two connect. A work-instruction is a rule for one task. When it dates, the procedure behind it explains how such tasks are done. When the procedure is not enough, a policy explains why. When policy is not enough, vision and principles say what the organisation is for. His example of a light switch in a power cut makes the point: the rule (press the switch) fails, and only the principle (we need light) tells you to find a torch.

The same posts crossmap Causal Layered Analysis onto SCAN: the litany of events in the Simple, systemic causes in the Complicated, worldviews in the Ambiguous, and myth and metaphor in the Not-known. At the moment of action, Graves notes, only the litany and the myth are available: the rule or the story.

Why this matters for a receiver

Notices, rent and insurance are rules. The approach to the director, the café owner and the residents after the fire is principle. Writing rules for the second is as much a mistake as improvising the first.

19 · Chaos

Into the void: three kinds of chaos

In brief

Graves distinguishes three kinds of chaos: the kaos before order, which is the source of everything new; perceived chaos, which is an imposed order breaking down; and end-chaos, after order, where nothing more can be done. He warns against treating them alike, and against filling the Not-known with ready-made solutions.

In a 2018 post on sensemaking in the void, Graves warns against solutioneering: arriving at the Not-known with an answer already chosen. The alternative is to seed the space with just enough structure and method. Too little and people drown; too much and the space becomes an echo chamber in which evidence is gathered to fit the policy. He describes the difference between entering the Not-known at leisure, from the Ambiguous, with time for questions, and being thrown into it at run-time across the edge of panic.

His 2020 post on three kinds of chaos adds a distinction. Kaos, before order, is infinite possibility. Perceived chaos is often an undesired order emerging as an imposed one breaks down. End-chaos, after order, is the exhaustion of possibility; pushing for spurious certainty, he argues, brings it closer. His image is a glider pilot looking for rising air rather than fighting the wind.

A further post on working the Not-known uses a game analogy: explorers, a guide who asks questions rather than giving answers, a scribe who records, and locals who know the ground, all working to a time limit for just enough detail.

Why this matters for a receiver

The night of the fire is perceived chaos: the building’s order breaking down. Empty unit 3 is kaos: an opening. A parade that the lender keeps trying to force to an early sale at a fixed price may be pushed toward end-chaos. Each needs a different response.

20 · The limits of automation

Flight 1549 and the limits of automation

In brief

Graves uses the Hudson river landing of US Airways 1549 in 2009, and the loss of Air France 447 in the same year, to show where automation helps and where it fails. Routine becomes emergency in seconds; what saves the day is preparation made long before and skill built in practice.

Flight 1549 was routine until a bird strike took out both engines. From then on the crew had seconds. Graves’s reading is that the outcome depended on things decided long before: the checklists, the aircraft’s systems, and the body-learning of hours in simulators. In SCAN terms the flight crossed the edge of panic, and what came back were options simple enough to act on.

In his 2014 post on learning and the limits of automation he notes that broadly similar automation was implicated in the loss of one aircraft and helped another land. His concern is that automation suits the Simple, erodes the skills needed beyond it, and leaves organisations with many people who can press buttons and few masters. He suggests that architects ask what their automation does not cover, how its failure would show, how manual operation and return would work, and how tacit knowledge is passed on between generations.

Why this matters for a receiver

Rent systems, bank feeds and compliance trackers are automation. They bank rent; they do not notice that the café owner has stopped coming in, or that the stair light has been out for three months. The receiver needs to know what the systems do not see, and who sees it.

21 · SCAN and Cynefin

SCAN and Cynefin: why there are two frames

In brief

SCAN grew from a dispute. In 2010 Graves published adaptations of Dave Snowden’s Cynefin framework; Snowden objected that a changed model should not carry Cynefin’s name. By late 2011 Graves had given his version a name of its own. The two frames ask different questions, and a receiver can use both.

In early 2010 Graves wrote a series of posts on complexity, chaos and enterprise architecture that adapted Cynefin to his own purposes, including crossmaps and a reading of chaos as something to be worked in. Snowden replied in a post later that year, arguing among other things that Graves conflated the chaotic and the complex in intervention, that Cynefin already provides for contained chaos, and that a new model should have a new name. In October 2011 Graves wrote that anything not conforming exactly to Cynefin should not be called Cynefin. A month later he introduced SCAN.

The resulting frames differ. Cynefin distinguishes kinds of system by their constraints and the relation between cause and effect. SCAN asks about certainty and time to action, and so is closer to the question of what this person can do now. SCAN’s Not-known is defined by the clock as much as by the system: the fire is Not-known because there is no time, whatever kind of system the parade is. Its Ambiguous is closer to what Cynefin calls complex. Treatise VI in this series applied Cynefin to this same parade; the two readings are meant to be set side by side, not merged.

Why this matters for a receiver

Ask two questions of a hard moment: is it hard because of the kind of system it is, or because of the clock? Cynefin helps with the first, SCAN with the second.

22 · Three dimensions

Three dimensions, and four kinds of knowing

In brief

In a 2011 post on domains and dimensions, Graves says the two-axis picture hides three dimensions: modality (true-or-false versus possibility and necessity), the time left before action, and repeatability (how much variety the context holds). Read together, they tell a receiver what kind of knowing each issue allows: known, knowable, discoverable, or only discoverable in the act.

The horizontal axis is really about the kind of reasoning that fits. On the left, the reasoning is two-valued: a thing is a fire door or it is not; rent is paid or it is not. On the right, the reasoning is modal: a tenant might stay, should stay if the terms are right, could stay if the bus stop comes back. Graves points out that the split between Simple and Not-known is itself a true-or-false cut made on a spectrum, so the frame contains its own recursion.

The vertical axis is time left before a decision has to become action. As it runs out, the options narrow, and deciding to do nothing is still a decision. Repeatability is the third dimension, the one most often forgotten. Graves distinguishes perfect repeatability, which exists only at the far left edge, from effective repeatability: the ability to absorb variation and still get the same result. A good agent achieves effective repeatability in rent collection not by doing the same thing to every tenant but by doing different things to get the same outcome.

Close to action, he argues, only modality can be seen. There is no time to ask how variable things are; only whether the situation fits a rule or does not. That is why the Simple and the Not-known form a matched pair at the moment of action, and the Complicated and the Ambiguous a matched pair at a distance from it. His 2011 post “Ensuring that the Simple stays simple” adds the practical corollary: keep the rules of the Simple genuinely simple, and give the people using them a clear way out when they stop fitting.

Four kinds of knowing on the parade

In his 2014 post on the knowable and the discoverable, Graves gives each domain its own kind of knowledge. Known (Simple): the date of the rent demand, the notice wording, the insurer’s telephone number. Knowable (Complicated): the valuation, the lease terms, the VAT position: it can be worked out given time and expertise. Discoverable (Ambiguous): whether vacant unit 3 will let as a meanwhile use, which can only be found out by testing. Discovering (Not-known): what the café owner will do when she hears of the appointment, which is only known as it happens. His warning is about staying too long above the plan-to-action line: analysis that never meets the world gets less done, not more.

Why this matters for a receiver

Lenders often ask for certainty about things that are only discoverable. Naming the kind of knowing honestly, in the first report, saves a later argument about why the forecast was wrong.

Key terms
Modality
Whether the reasoning is two-valued (is, is not) or about possibility (might, should, could).
Effective repeatability
Getting the same result despite variation, often by varying what is done.
Known, knowable, discoverable, discovering
The four kinds of knowledge that belong to the four domains.
23 · Decision paths

Belief, assertion, use and faith: the paths between decisions

In brief

In his 2012 series on linking intent and action, Graves maps four kinds of decision onto the frame: belief (Simple), assertion (Complicated), use (Ambiguous) and faith (Not-known). Paths up and down, or side to side, work. Paths across the diagonal, which change the kind of certainty and the distance from action in a single move, do not.

Belief and assertion, in his account, are driven by a wish for certainty and control; use and faith by experience and trust. Each needs its own tools for sensing and for deciding: rule-sets and checklists for belief; algorithms and analytics for assertion; patterns, guidelines and tests for use; principles, skill and designs that fail safely for faith. An organisation that supplies only the first two, as most do, leaves its people to improvise the other two alone.

The functional paths each change one thing. Analysis is packaged into rules for action, and the results of action go back to revise the analysis. Questions analysis cannot settle go to experiment, and firm results come back as theory: Graves calls this the meeting of praxis and pragmatics. Patterns and principles prepared at leisure go into the unknown as seeds, and what is found there comes back as material for the next test. At the moment of action the Simple and the Not-known swap control under strain: across the edge of panic, and back again.

He calls the diagonals dysfunctional. An analysis applied directly to the unknown as if it were certain is one: his examples include business process re-engineering applied to people, and Six Sigma used outside contexts of near-identical events. An experiment’s “it depends” passed straight to someone who needs a rule is the other. His pilot example shows the functional alternative. The radio fails during the pre-take-off checklist; there is a flash of panic; the pilot stops and runs quick tests above the line, finds a simple fix, and returns to the checklist. Two steps, not one jump.

Decision-physics

Graves compares decision-making to physics. Most considered decisions, from strategy down to operations, behave in a “Newtonian” middle range. At very long timescales, assumptions such as ownership start to break down. At the moment of action, things behave more like quantum physics: what happens there is real, and everything else is abstraction. Rationality belongs to the middle range. At the moment of action decisions are emotional, made on belief or faith, which is why the parade’s night of the fire went as it did whatever the plan said.

Why this matters for a receiver

The commonest diagonal on an appointment is the valuation read out to a distressed director as if it settled things. Go across first (what would make this usable?), then down.

Key terms
Belief, assertion, use, faith
Decisions from rules, from analysis, from what has been tried, and from principle.
Dysfunctional diagonal
A move that changes both certainty and distance from action at once.
Praxis and pragmatics
Theory turned into practice, and practice distilled back into theory.
24 · The swamp

Four modes of sensemaking, and seven sins

In brief

One of SCAN’s oldest roots is Lionel Snell’s image of reality as a swamp, which Graves used in his work on the disciplines of sensemaking. There are four modes: the Believer (inner truth), the Scientist (outer truth), the Technologist (outer value) and the Artist (inner value). Each has its own rules of evidence, warning signs and bridges to the others. Seven common “sins” break the discipline.

The modes lie on the same frame. Truth is on the left of the red line and value on the right. Inner modes are below the green line and work at the moment of action; outer modes are above it and need time. The Believer holds focus and works from a defined list. The Scientist checks facts against shared standards and asks whether others would see the same. The Technologist asks what is useful here, trades off risk and works in probabilities. The Artist notices: the unexpected reply, the side-feeling, the first time.

None is wrong, and nobody lives in one. Graves’s point is that each has a characteristic loop (sense, categorise, act; enquire, sense, analyse; experiment, sense, evaluate; act or pause, sense, reflect) and characteristic warning signs. The Scientist who says “obviously” has become attached to a fact. The Technologist who says “same as last time” has stopped testing. The Artist who says “this proves…” has mistaken a feeling for evidence. The Believer who says “true for me, so true for everyone” has become what Graves calls the Priest, turning a personal certainty into rules for others.

The seven sins of dubious discipline

Graves adapted the list from his and Liz Poraj-Wilczynska’s work on dowsing, and later wrote a series on it. In this treatise’s wording: Hype Hubris: marketing passed off as expertise. Golden-Age Game: the claim that some past or secret method had the answer. Newage Nuisance: wilful neglect of discipline in the name of openness. Meaning Mistake: ideas used half-formed, or over-elaborated until they mean nothing. Possession Problem: claiming to own the truth because uncertainty is frightening. Reality Risk: forgetting that in some kinds of sensemaking the real and the imagined are hard to tell apart. Lost in the Learning Labyrinth: trying to skip the stages of learning a skill, which can only be walked in order.

Each sin has its own receivership form. Hype Hubris is the pitch that promises a sale in eight weeks. The Golden-Age Game is “this is how we did it in 2009”. The Possession Problem is the agent who cannot say “I don’t know”. Graves’s later post links the sins to the Hype Cycle, and another to Boyd’s OODA: the sins tend to appear at the points where a loop is cut short.

Why this matters for a receiver

Most disputes on an appointment are between modes, not people: the valuer’s truth against the agent’s usefulness against the director’s feeling. Naming the mode lowers the temperature and points to the right bridge question.

Key terms
Believer, Scientist, Technologist, Artist
Inner truth, outer truth, outer value, inner value.
Bridge question
A question that moves deliberately from one mode to another.
Metadiscipline
Graves’s “discipline of discipline”: knowing which mode you are in.
25 · Four layers

Four layers of a stakeholder’s story

In brief

Graves crossmaps Sohail Inayatullah’s Causal Layered Analysis (CLA) onto SCAN, relabelling its four layers Simple (the litany), Study (systemic causes), Search (worldview) and Story (myth and metaphor). The surprise is the vertical axis: study and search need time. At the moment of action people act only from the litany or the deep story.

CLA treats any issue as four layers. The litany is what people say on the surface, often about what others should do. The systemic layer gives the causes and data that justify it. The worldview layer is the frame that decides which facts count. The deepest layer is myth and metaphor: the story that makes things feel right or wrong. Inayatullah’s point is that problems are often solved at the wrong layer: adding data does nothing if the disagreement is about worldview.

Graves’s addition, in his 2012 and 2014 posts, is to put the layers on SCAN’s axes. Depth becomes the certainty axis, and the distance from action matters. Objectivity, he notes, is a luxury that needs distance from the fray. When people are in the action, they have only overt surface rules and the usually unconscious deep story. He also names a failure, the Simplistic mode: deep assumptions presented as if they were simple rules. Part of the purpose of CLA, and of tools such as Nigel Green’s VPEC-T, is to surface those assumptions so that choices made under pressure are choices.

He gives Green’s work with the justice system as an example: police and prosecutors had clashing deep stories about speed and paperwork. Neither was wrong. The way forward was to find the point where both stories wanted the same outcome. The instrument below does the same for five people on the parade.

Why this matters for a receiver

The director’s litany (“they’ve stolen my building”) will not change with a better spreadsheet. It changes, if at all, at the level of story, and the receiver’s best opening is the outcome both stories want.

Litany
The surface account, often a complaint.
Worldview
The frame that decides what counts as fact.
Simplistic mode
Unexamined deep story presented as simple rules.
26 · Modal lists

Requirements and approved lists: is, sometimes, believed, unknown

In brief

In two of his first SCAN examples, from 2011, Graves applies the frame to requirements and to reference lists. Requirements are “shall” on the left and “should” on the right. A reference list needs four answers, not two: is-a, is-sometimes-a, is-believed-to-work, and none-of-the-above. With no time, both collapse back to two.

Any new system, he says, starts in the Not-known. Writing requirements is pulling items out of it. Rules, regulations and standards are Simple and become “shall”. Things that are complicated but must be delivered also become “shall”, after some thought. Things that are ambiguous become “should”. He quotes Cynthia Kurtz on the effect of time: given ten years anything can be made to work; given ten minutes it had better be simple. Under pressure, Complicated items are forced into “shall” and Ambiguous ones drop to a weaker “should”, or stay unknown until the last moment.

Most reference lists, he notes, only say “this is what we use”. That provokes fights with whoever polices the list. When there is time, a list with modality works better: what we use; what we can use at a known extra cost; what we are told works but have not tried; and what we know almost nothing about. Over time, repeated sorting shows items moving from “believed” to “sometimes” to “is”, and shows which never move.

The receiver’s instruction letter

A receiver’s first instructions to an agent are a requirements document. The insurance, the notices and the rent account are “shall”. The soft-market test is “should”: it can be specified only as an experiment with an exit condition. Writing it as “shall” promises a certainty nobody has; writing the insurance as “should” is negligent.

Why this matters for a receiver

Panels of agents, valuers and contractors are reference lists. Sorting them four ways tells the lender which instructions are routine, which cost more, which buy information and which carry risk.

Key terms
Shall, should, could
Requirements by certainty; “could” slides easily into wishful thinking.
Modal reference list
A list that records how sure we are, not just yes or no.
27 · Real time

An architecture for sensemaking in real time

In brief

Graves’s four-part series on sensemaking in enterprise architecture (2011) ends with a practical list. Support the Simple with work instructions, checklists, practice and deliberate mismatch. Support the Not-so-simple with the discipline to hold off panic, open observation, improvisation and seeding with principles. Then design for both: name the kind of decision each context needs, set limits on every rule, and give people a way out.

At the Simple end, he says, the considered tactic is sense, categorise, respond. The categories have to come from somewhere. He recalls a security organisation that said it had a rule for everything; asked what happened when something new came up, it said it would make up another rule, after the event. During the event, it had nothing. Checklists help, and so does repetition until the response becomes bodily. So does a deliberate mismatch, a trigger that says “this no longer fits”, sending the work to the other side.

At the Not-so-simple end, the considered tactic is act, sense, respond: do something to get information, then either step back and think or collapse back to a rule. Neither works in real time. His example is juggling: simple in principle, but every variation has to be absorbed as it happens, and thinking too hard drops the ball. The techniques here are different: the discipline to stay calm, open observation that lets the situation be seen as it is, improvisation (“yes, and”), and seeding the space with intention, images, vision and values in advance.

The balance, he says, is this. The moment you need to make sense of something, you are on the Not-simple side. Reliability and speed come from pushing work back to the Simple; flexibility comes from keeping access to the Not-simple. Not-simple techniques depend on skill, so the architecture has to say where skill is needed and how it will be available.

Checklist or work instruction

Graves separates the two. A work instruction is a fixed sequence for something known; it is followed. A checklist tests conditions that need not be in order, so that something known to go wrong does not. The Cessna checklist he discusses has a routine side, long and done before or between phases, and an emergency side, short, ordered and read under pressure. The parade’s four checklists follow the same split. Principles, too, need structure to be usable: a name, a statement, a rationale and implications (from TOGAF), and Graves adds consequences: what happens if it is not applied, and how to tell if it is misapplied.

Documenting the Not-known

In 2018 Graves and the designer Joseph Chittenden found that exploration needs “just enough” structure, and that what is enough changes by phase. They proposed three kinds of document: a tightly structured start-of-phase brief, a loose visual canvas for the messy middle, and a tightly structured end-of-phase summary for review. On the parade: the appointment brief; a wall sheet for the first fortnight’s findings; the thirty-day report to the lender.

Co-inspiring in the chaotic

Graves cites Kevin Smith on traffic in Manila and Saigon: two principles (if you see a gap in the direction you want to go, take it; don’t hit anything) and almost no anger, because nobody is breaking a rule. Gridlock, in that account, comes from trying to control the flow. On the night of the fire, the residents on the forecourt worked much like that once two principles were given: stay together, keep the road clear.

He also offers what he calls the Sensemaker’s Mantra, in three parts. “I don’t know”, but I know how to find out. “It depends”, and I know how to find out what it depends on. “Just enough detail”, and I know what level of detail will be needed.

Why this matters for a receiver

Most appointment manuals are all Simple. The list below is a gap analysis: for each part of the appointment, which kind of decision does it need, and who has the skill to make it at three in the morning?

Key terms
Deliberate mismatch
A trigger that sends work from rules to judgement.
Seeding
Preparing principles and images in advance of the unknown.
Just-enough structure
Documentation whose rigidity matches the phase.
28 · People

Managers, masters and the Demoralised Man

In brief

Several of Graves’s posts read organisations through SCAN. Taylorism assumes managers can strip uncertainty out of work, but depends on masters who resolve it at run time and offers no way to grow them. Five levels of skill, from Robot to Master, map onto the frame and onto the layers of a quality system. When rules overrule ethics, people become demoralised in two senses, and a spiral begins.

His 2018 post on managers and masters starts from Taylor’s split: managers think, workers do. The split assumes every uncertainty can be turned into an instruction. But reducing uncertainty means facing it, so pure Taylorism cannot deliver itself. It relies on masters, who resolve the surprises at run time. Promoting them to management takes them away from run time and asks for different skills. The frame also shows a gap in the upper right: roles for working with real uncertainty away from the pressure of run time, which neither managers nor masters fill. In receivership, that gap is usually where strategy is lost.

Graves gives five levels of skill. A Robot follows rules and cannot change them. A Trainee works mostly from rules, close to action, and needs supervision. An Apprentice can handle analysis with feedback. A Journeyman works with ambiguity and context. A Master works with what is unique, at run time, holding the whole picture. He maps these to the layers of an ISO 9000 quality system: work instructions are for Robots and Trainees; procedures are used by Apprentices and written by Journeymen; policy is used by Journeymen and written by Masters. His caution: it is not really a skill unless there is some uncertainty, and a master in one skill is a trainee in another.

The Demoralised Man

In a 2016 post Graves argues that a profession is defined by commitment to shared values, codified as ethics and made usable as principles. He tells of an engineering laboratory where a project manager ranked protecting an irreplaceable test article above staff safety; a senior engineer pointed out that the engineers’ ethics put people first and they would all have to resign. Management backed down. When rules overrule ethics, people are de-moralised (their ethics set aside) and de-moraled (their morale destroyed). The first causes the second, and the result is a spiral: tighter controls, disengagement, falling quality, rising cost, tighter controls again. The way out is clear purpose, values and ethics for decisions where rules do not reach.

Pay, targets and the NHS

His 2013 post on the NHS applies the same reading to a public service. More managers, tighter targets and pay tied to performance, he argues, are each known causes of the problems they are meant to fix: targets get gamed, and above a basic level more pay does not improve skilled work. His alternative is whole-enterprise: start from purpose; acknowledge that most of the work is unique and uncertain, so methods built for sameness do not fit; map with SCAN where standardisation can and cannot be used; measure, rather than set targets; and rebalance responsibilities. He cites a BMJ argument that military rather than civil aviation is the better model for safer healthcare, and prefers Hickam’s dictum (a patient may have several conditions at once) to Occam’s razor.

The echo chamber

In his 2015 series on services and disservices, Graves describes how evidence gets filtered by belief into “policy-based evidence”, and names two causes of a loud echo chamber: fear (of losing control, when things stop working) and over-investment in a belief (financial, structural or emotional). The remedies differ. For fear: let go of control and use Not-known tactics, such as a reframe, rich randomness or guiding principles. For over-investment: a conscious choice to write the investment down.

Why this matters for a receiver

The lender’s recovery team, the agent’s property managers and the receiver’s own juniors each sit at a level of skill. Instructions written for Robots, given to a Journeyman, waste them; instructions written for Journeymen, given to a Trainee, are dangerous.

Key terms
Robot to Master
Five levels of skill, by how much uncertainty each can handle.
De-moralised
Ethics set aside by rules; morale follows.
Policy-based evidence
Evidence selected to fit a belief.
29 · Wild problems

Wild problems, requisite fuzziness and coincidensity

In brief

From Graves’s complexity anthology: “wicked” problems are better called wild, and tame problems are wild ones with constraints added. Every rule needs as much fuzziness as the context it governs. Serendipity can be designed for. And effectiveness has five strands, two of which belong to order, two to unorder, and one to both.

In “Not so wicked” (2014) Graves takes Jeffrey Conklin’s list of the features of wicked problems and asks whether “wicked” is the right word. He prefers “wild”. Tame problems, he argues, are special cases of wild ones, with conditions imposed like fences or bridles. The split is recursive: every wild problem has tame parts, and every tame one has wild parts. He tells of a “buffalo bridle”: not a thing you own but a way of setting things up so that the animal goes where you want without apparent force. That is close to what a good receiver does with a parade.

His 2013 post on requisite fuzziness extends Ashby’s law of requisite variety. A control system that handles less variety than its context cannot control it. In the same way, a rule, pattern or principle that allows less variance than the context needs will fail. His example is a driving rule, which has to allow for the car, distractions, the driver’s experience and other drivers. Reducing a qualitative requirement to a metric can remove exactly the fuzziness it needs.

He takes the word coincidensity from Matt Biddulph: the likelihood of serendipity, the density of unplanned coincidences. Structures can be designed to raise it or lower it. His 2012 post on John Kay’s Obliquity adds that indirect paths briefly throw a goal-seeking context into unorder, usually only to the level of worldview, and that purpose still matters as the anchor.

Five strands of effectiveness

In “Order, unorder and effectiveness” (2012) Graves splits effectiveness into five: efficient (use of resources, waste), reliable (consistent, self-correcting), elegant (clear, simple, humane), appropriate (fit to purpose) and integrated (each part supports the others). Efficient and reliable are mostly order; elegant and appropriate mostly unorder; integrated bridges both. On the parade: rent banking is efficient and reliable; the way the residents are told about the fire must be elegant; the meanwhile licence must be appropriate; and the whole appointment must be integrated, or one strand undoes another.

Similar is not the same

Graves uses Hickam’s dictum against Occam’s razor: a case may have several causes at once. He notes Australian research on ageing aircraft, where corrosion, fatigue and damage interact differently in every airframe. Six shops and eight flats on Station Road are not fourteen instances of one thing. In “When identical is not the same as equal” he adds that identical provision can produce unequal outcomes: the same notice sent to every tenant is not the same treatment for each.

Why this matters for a receiver

Lenders want tame problems. A receiver serves them best by fencing what can be fenced, saying clearly what cannot, and designing the rest so that good chances are more likely.

Key terms
Wild problem
Graves’s preferred name for a wicked problem; tame problems are constrained wild ones.
Requisite fuzziness
The variance a rule must allow to fit its context.
Coincidensity
The density of unplanned coincidences that make serendipity likely.
30 · The appointment

Play the appointment through SCAN

In brief

Ten moments from the parade appointment, from appointment morning to the review after the fire. Each moment belongs to one SCAN domain. Choose a tactic at each, and see whether it fits.

Play once as you would actually act. Then run the three presets, the rule-follower, the analyst and the domain-matcher, and compare them in the history. The rule-follower does well on appointment morning and on the night of the fire, and badly whenever the moment is ambiguous. The analyst does well whenever there is time, and badly at the moment of action. Only matching the tactic to the domain does well throughout. The scoring is this treatise’s judgement, offered to be argued with.

Why this matters for a receiver

Most receivers have a preferred style. The game shows where that style costs them.
